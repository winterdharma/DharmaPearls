The gods and I will be right behind you!”</td>

    <td class='ch' title='t1.1.63a16'>爾時，世尊從三昧起，告般遮翼言： 「善哉！善哉！般遮翼，汝能以清淨音和琉璃琴稱讚如來。 琴聲、汝音，不長不短，悲和哀婉，感動人心。 汝琴所奏，眾義備有，亦說欲縛，亦說梵行，亦說沙門，亦說涅槃。」</td>
    <td id='p11'>11. The Bhagavān then roused from his <em>samādhi</em> and told Pañcaśikha, “Good, Pañcaśikha, good! You’re able to praise the Tathāgata with your clear voice and harmonious cymophane lute. The sound of both your lute and voice are neither long or short, and their compassion and gracefulness moves people’s hearts. Your song is replete with many meanings and explains the bonds of desire, the religious life, the ascetic, and <em>nirvāṇa</em>!”</td>

You’ve always been faithful and close to the Tathāgata. I think I’d like to be friends with you.’ Bhagavān, after speaking with her that one time, I never spoke with her again.”</td>

    <td class='ch' title='t1.1.63b19'>帝釋白佛言：「一時，佛在舍衛國婆羅<!-- [10] -->門舍，爾時世尊入火焰三昧，我時以少因緣，乘千輻寶車，詣毘樓勒天王所，於空中過，見一天女叉手在世尊前立，我尋語彼女言：『若世尊三昧起者，汝當稱我名字，問訊世尊：起居輕利，遊步強耶？』不審彼女後竟為我達此心不？世尊！寧能憶此事不？」</td>
    <td id='p22'>22. Lord Śakra said to the Buddha, “One time, the Buddha was staying at a priest’s residence in Śrāvastī. At the time, the Bhagavān had entered the samādhi of blazing fire. For some reason, I was riding my thousand-spoked treasure chariot to visit Virūḍhaka. As I went by in the sky, I saw a goddess standing with her palms together in front of the Bhagavān. I immediately said to that goddess, ‘If the Bhagavān rouses from samādhi, you should exchange these greetings with the Bhagavān in my name: “Has your daily life been easy? Are you in good health?”’ I wondered later if she had conveyed this thought for me. Bhagavān, do you recall if she did?”</td>
  </tr>

    <td id='p23'>23. The Buddha said, “I do remember it. That goddess immediately put the question to me with her goddess’s voice, and I roused from samādhi. It was like the sound of a woman’s chariot.”</td>

    <td class='ch' title='t1.1.63b27'>[0063b27] 帝釋白佛言：「昔者，我以少緣，與忉利諸天集在法堂，彼諸舊天皆作是言：『若如來出世，增益諸天眾，減損<!-- [11] -->阿須輪眾。』今我躬見世尊，躬見自知，躬自作證，如來、至真出現於世，增益諸天眾，減損阿須輪眾。</td>
    <td id='p24'>24. Lord Śakra said to the Buddha, “In the past, I held a meeting for some reason with the Trāyastriṃśa gods in the Dharma [Meeting] Hall. Those gods back then had said, ‘If a Tathāgata arises in the world, the host of gods will increase and the host of asuras will diminish!’ Now that I see the Bhagavān in person, I personally see and know it myself. I’ve realized it myself: ‘The Tathāgata, the Arhat, has arisen in the world, and the host of gods are increasing, and the host of asuras are diminishing!’</td>

<h3 id="notes">Notes</h3>

<ol class="notes-list">
<li id="n1"><p>This is <em>sūtra</em> no. 14 of the <cite>Dīrgha Āgama</cite> (<a href="https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/T01n0001_p0062b28" target="_blank">T1.1.62b28-6a3</a>). Parallels for this <em>sūtra</em> include <a href="https://suttacentral.net/dn21" target="_blank">DN 21</a>, <a href="https://suttacentral.net/ma134" target="_blank">MĀ 134</a>, <a href="https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/T0015_001" target="_blank">T15</a>, <a href="https://cbetaonline.dila.edu.tw/zh/T04n0203_p0476a17" target="_blank">T203.73</a>, and a Sanskrit fragment (<a href="https://suttacentral.net/sf241/san/waldschmidt" target="_blank">SF 241</a>. [<a href="#ref1">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n2"><p><em>north of the village … Mount Vaidehaka</em>. C. <span class="ch">摩竭國菴婆羅村北，毘陀山因陀娑羅窟中</span>, P. <em>magadhesu viharati, pācīnato rājagahassa ambasaṇḍā nāma brāhmaṇagāmo, tassuttarato vediyake pabbate indasālaguhāyaṁ</em>. All of the parallels agree more-or-less with this location information, which is somewhat convoluted compared to most Buddhist <em>sūtra</em>s. The fact that the extant recensions agree as much as they do shows that this text was quite stable across traditions. A couple of the C. terms here are unclear until we peruse sources in other languages.</p>
<p><em>village of Āmra</em>. C. <span class="ch">菴婆羅村</span> (EMC. •əm-bua-la + “village” = G. <em>*aṃbragrama</em>), P. <em>ambasaṇḍā nāma brāhmaṇagāmo</em>. Here, the name of the village was shortened to simply S. <em>āmra</em> (“mango”), which in G. was sometimes written <em>aṃbra</em>. In light of this, it seems likely to me that <span class="ch">婆羅</span> was intended to translit. <em>-bra</em>, not <em>-bara</em> or <em>-bala</em>. There is also no mention of brahmins in this version. T15 and T203 both agree with the P. version of this placename, and MĀ 134 only omits mention of brahmins. All three appear to translit. and/or trans. S. <em>āmraṣaṇḍā</em> (“mango grove”).</p>
<p><em>Mount Vaidehaka</em>. C. <span class="ch">毘陀山</span> (EMC. bii-da + “mountain” = G. <em>vida- pavada</em>), P. <em>vediyaka pabbata</em>, S. <em>vaidehaka parvata</em>. I’ve adopted the S. for this mountain’s name, which is attested in the S. fragment edited by Waldschimdt. The version here resembles it, but it appears the end has been clipped off. MĀ 134 translit. something like G. <em>vedadi</em> (= S. <em>vetti</em>, “knows”), T15 translit. G. <em>vedehi</em>, and T203 agrees with T15. Thus, all the parallels agree somehwhat on the first two syllables, but the names had variable endings. It was not a stable placename.</p>
<p><em>Indraśaila Cave</em>. C. <span class="ch">因陀娑羅窟</span> (EMC. •iĕn-da-sa-la = G. <em>*iṃdasala</em>), P. <em>indasālaguhā</em>, S. <em>indraśailaguhā</em>. This placename is well known in later P. and S. sources as a hill cave located somewhere outside of Rājagṛha. What’s most remarkable here is that the translit. <span class="ch">因陀娑羅</span> is almost exactly the same pronunciation as P. <em>indasāla</em>, even though normally P. <em>inda</em> would become G. <em>iṃdra</em>. MĀ 134 has Indra’s Cave (<span class="ch">因陀羅石室</span>), T15 has Lord Śakra’s Crag (<span class="ch">帝釋巖</span>), and T203 simply says he was in a cave on Mount Vedehi (<span class="ch">毘提醯山石窟之中</span>). These variations suggest that the later tradition is not necessarily the original one, but DĀ 14 certainly agrees with it here. [<a href="#ref2">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n3"><p>In DN 21, Sakka approaches the gods of the Tāvatiṁsa Heaven rather than the gods approaching him. However, the other three C. parallels all lack this exchange. Instead, Śakra approaches Pañcaśikha after hearing that the Buddha was staying at the cave on Mount Vaidehaka. The S. fragment appears to proceed in the same fashion as the other C. parallels, but the introduction is missing, so it’s not entirely clear which event comes first in that version. [<a href="#ref3">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n4"><p>gandharva <em>Pañcaśikha</em>. C. <span class="ch">執樂神般遮翼</span>, P. <em>pañcasikha gandhabbadevaputta</em>, S. <em>paṁcaśikha gandharvaputra</em>. The C. here trans. <em>gandharva</em> as music (<span class="ch">樂</span>) bearing (<span class="ch">執</span>) spirit (<span class="ch">神</span>). Pañcaśikha is translit. as EMC. buan-tʃɪă-yiək, which perhaps renders something like Pr. <em>paṃcayik[ha]</em>, where the <em>-ś-</em> had become a <em>-y-</em> or gone silent. Lit., S. <em>pañcaśikha</em> is generally considered to mean “five crests,” though MĀ 134 and T15 both translate <em>śikha</em> as “topknot.” [<a href="#ref4">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n5"><p><em>the Bhagavān had entered the <em>samādhi</em> of blazing fire</em>. This attribution of the fiery light on the mountain to the Buddha is only found in this version of the <em>sūtra</em>. All the others, including DN 21, imply that it was the glow of the assembly of gods. [<a href="#ref5">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n6"><p><em>Bhadrā … your father</em>. Bhadrā was the daughter of the king of <em>gandharva</em>s. Śakra indicates at the conclusion of the present <em>sūtra</em> that Pañcaśikha was her suitor. [<a href="#ref6">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n7"><p>This verse is nearly identical to verse 11 in DN 21. Verse 7 in MĀ 134 is similar but references a sage attaining awakening in the second couplet. In T15, verse 4 is changed even more, referencing an <em>arhat</em> delighting in the way of peace. The first couplet of verse 2 in T203.73 also references an <em>arhat</em>’s love of the Dharma. Verse 2 in the Sanskrit fragment reads very similar to MĀ 134. [<a href="#ref7">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n8"><p>This verse is very similar to verse 6 in DN 21, but here the animal hooked is an elephant, not a fish. In T15, the second couplet of verse 3 includes the example of an elephant controlled with a hook, but not in the same context. The first verse in T203.73 likewise uses the image of an elephant and a hook, but there it’s a rutting elephant that can’t be controlled in that way. [<a href="#ref8">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n9"><p>This and the previous verse contain most of the similes of relief found in verses 2 and 3 of DN 21: the cool wind, cool drink of water, water dousing fire, medicine for the sick, food for the starving, and the Dharma to an <em>arhat</em>. Verse 2 and 9 of MĀ 134 mention all of these similes, too. T15 mentions the wind, drink, medicine, food, and the <em>arhat</em> in verses 2-4, but there’s more variation evident. Several of these similes also occur in T203.73 verses 3 and 4: the wind, drink, <em>arhat</em>, medicine, and food. Instead of a fire, Pañcaśikha’s fever is doused. [<a href="#ref9">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n10"><p>This verse has some similarity with verse 5 of DN 21, but there the elephant defeats weapons rather than a handler’s hook, and the second couplet references being crazy about a woman’s attractiveness. Verse 5 of MĀ 134 instead references a shackled elephant that powerless, which is likened to the woman not responding to the suitor’s efforts. Verse 1 in T203 has a simile of a maddened elephant that can’t be controlled with the hook. [<a href="#ref10">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n11"><p>Verse 4 in both DN 21 and MĀ 134 present similes of elephants refreshing themselves in ponds. All three versions mention water plants in the pond and the elephants bathing, but only DN 21 turns this into a simile about Pancasikha’s desire. [<a href="#ref11">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n12"><p>This verse corresponds to verse 9 in both DN 21 and MĀ 134 and verse 3 in T15. Strangely, DN 21 repeats this verse in verse 4 by changing the second line to a different kind of merit. [<a href="#ref12">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n13"><p>This verse, with a sentiment so universal to the human condition of being in love, is not found in DN 21, but it matches verse 10 in MĀ 134 quite well. One wonders is the second verse on sharing merits in DN 21 hasn’t overwritten it. [<a href="#ref13">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n14"><p>This concluding verse matches verse 13 in DN 21 and verse 5 in T15. The conclusion in MĀ 134 jarringly switches to praising the Buddha, and T203 doesn’t conclude with a request for Bhadrā’s hand. [<a href="#ref14">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n15"><p><em>the Ajapāla Nigrodha tree on the bank of the Nairañjanā River in Uruvilvā</em>. C. <span class="ch">鬱鞞羅、尼連禪水邊，阿遊波陀、尼俱律樹</span>, P. <em>uruvelāyaṁ viharati najjā nerañjarāya tīre ajapālanigrodhe</em>. The C. transliterations of these placenames generally agree with DN 21, with some of the usual differences in pronunciation found in other Prakrit languages.</p>
<p><em></em>. C. <span class="ch">阿遊波陀</span> (EMC. •a-yiəu-pua-da = <em>ayopada</em>?), P. <em>ajapāla</em>. In many G. texts, <em>j</em> > <em>y</em> and <em>l</em> > <em>d</em> when compared to Sanskrit or Pali, so this variation in pronunciation here is not unexpected. The word is interpreted to mean a goatherd in P. sources (i.e., <em>aja</em> + <em>pāla</em> = "goat protector"), but there is no attestation in G. that I have found to confirm the same reading here. I have adopted the S. reading as per my usual policy.</p>
<p><em>banyan tree</em>. C. <span class="ch">尼俱律樹</span> (EMC. ṇɪi-kɪu-lɪuĕt + “tree” = nigro[dha]), P. <em>nigrodha</em>, S. <em>nyagrodha</em>. Here, <span class="ch">俱律</span> represent the syllable <em>-kro-</em>, and the final syllable has been dropped.</p>
<p><em>beside the Nairañjanā River</em>. C. <span class="ch">尼連禪水邊</span> (EMC. ṇɪi-lɪɛn-ʒɪɛn + “riverside” = niranjan[a]?), P. <em>nerañjarā</em>, S. <em>nairañjanā</em>. The translit. here matches the S. better than it does the P.</p>
<p><em>Uruvilvā</em>. C. <span class="ch">鬱鞞羅</span> (EMC. •ɪuət-bei-la = [ur]uvela?), P. <em>uruvelā</em>, S. <em>uruvilvā</em>. Lacking a G. or other Prakrit attestation, I can only guess that the initial <em>ur-</em> has been omitted from the translit. here. [<a href="#ref15">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n16"><p><em>Śikhaṇḍin</em>. C. 尸漢陀 (EMC. ṣï-han-da = <em>śihanda</em>), P. <em>sikhaṇḍī</em>, S. <em>śikhaṇḍin</em>. Here, the <em>-kh-</em> in this name has weakened to <em>-h-</em>, which is common in G. This character is called a general of the gods here. In the P. tradition, he was the son of a heavenly charioteer, Mātali, who was the love interest of Bhadda in a triangle involving Pañcaśikha. [<a href="#ref16">back</a>]</p></li>
<li id="n17"><p><em>error</em>. C. <span class="ch">調戲</span>. This is presumably a translation of S. <em>prapañca</em> (P. <em>papañca</em>). This is a difficult term to pin down in Indic sources, being variously interpreted as proliferation, activity, and error or false ideas. The Chinese translation adds to the difficulty because it could be read as restlessness or agitation (S. <em>auddhatya</em>, P. <em>uddhacca</em>) but can mean reckless or wild behavior. Given the way the term appears to take on both epistemological and moral meanings in the passages that follow, I’ve opted for “error” as a translation, assuming the original was indeed S. <em>prapañca</em>. The Japanese translators agree, translating it as “false ideas.” [<a href="#ref17">back</a>]</p></li>
</ol>
